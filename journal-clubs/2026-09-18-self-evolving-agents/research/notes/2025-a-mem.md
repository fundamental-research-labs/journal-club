## Question and methods

A-MEM represents an interaction as a structured note with context, keywords, tags and an embedding (§3.1). Embedding retrieval proposes nearby memories, and an LLM decides meaningful links (§3.2). A new note can trigger revisions to earlier notes' attributes (§3.3); retrieval can access related notes through the resulting links (§3.4). The editable state is the memory network, not the base model's weights.

## Results and evidence

The original evaluates memory-dependent question answering across language-model backbones; the citation repair makes no new quantitative claim from those experiments. Separately, AgentStream explicitly reimplements A-Mem as a tested memory method (§4 and Tables 5–6). Its mixed-stream comparison is AgentStream's result, not a result attributed to the original A-MEM paper.

## Appraisal and limitations

A-MEM supplies a concrete retrieval-based alternative to an accumulated context playbook. ACE uses the memory-entry analogy (§3.1), while MemSkill and SelfMem directly discuss or compare memory construction approaches. Dependence on the underlying LLM and text-only experiments limit generality (§6). More structured links do not alone prove better task-agent learning, and AgentStream's multi-method comparison does not isolate linking as a cause.

## Discussion and follow-up

Which helps on a new task: the stored note, its rewritten attributes, or traversing its links? Compare those contributions with the same underlying experience and retrieval budget.
