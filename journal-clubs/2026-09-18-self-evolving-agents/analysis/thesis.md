# Self-evolving agents: learning from experience beyond a single task

## Introduction

An agent that encounters the same kind of problem repeatedly should be able to use earlier experience to improve its later decisions. For language-model agents, that experience can take several forms: a remembered failure, a reusable program, a revised instruction, or training data that changes the model itself. Research on self-evolving agents asks how to produce and retain these changes automatically. The central question is whether the resulting changes help on subsequent work, and under what conditions that benefit persists. Early systems already illustrate why the distinction matters: Reflexion uses verbal feedback to improve repeated attempts, whereas Voyager builds a library of executable skills for reuse during continued exploration. These are related forms of adaptation, but success on another attempt and transfer to a new task require different evidence. [Reflexion, §§3–4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366v4); [Voyager, §§2–3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16291).

We use *self-evolution* to describe a process in which experience changes retained state that influences subsequent behavior. The term does not imply that every component is autonomous: a system may generate its own updates while relying on a fixed model, externally supplied feedback, and a human-designed selection rule. The reviewed work extends this design space from memories and skills to prompts, model updates, generated curricula, and the code that proposes further improvements. These directions overlap historically; STOP already optimized an improver's own code in 2023. What distinguishes them is where experience is stored, how an update is selected, and what remains fixed. [STOP, §3 and Algorithm 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02304v3); [GEPA, §3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19457v2); [SEAL, §3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10943v2).

Our reading of this literature is that agents can learn useful procedures and update strategies within bounded settings, while the durability and economic value of those improvements remain conditional. Positive persistence controls show that retained experience can matter. Comparisons with additional sampling, strong static procedures, and longer task streams show why a rising score alone is insufficient. This motivates a common standard across otherwise different methods: evaluate whether an accepted change improves later work relative to credible alternatives, including its costs and regressions. Sustained, general recursive improvement requires the further demonstration that the improvement procedure itself becomes more effective across tasks and successive cycles.

## Related work

A first family of methods retains experience outside the model's weights. Reflexion stores textual feedback for later trials, while Voyager stores executable procedures. WikiSkill separates the knowledge accumulated during skill development from the skills ultimately selected for execution, allowing lessons to survive even when a proposed edit is rejected. This distinction makes the management of experience an explicit part of learning: retaining only successful artifacts can discard useful information about unsuccessful alternatives. In WikiSkill's experiments, Qwen-3.5-9B macro accuracy increases from 29.9 to 47.4 across five benchmarks with held-out splits and three evolution runs. The result supports this approach under the reported protocol; small validation sets, direct skill injection, and a regression for a smaller model on OfficeQA limit broader conclusions. [WikiSkill, §§3–4, Table 1 and Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27454v1).

Prompt and harness optimization changes the instructions and software that organize an agent's work. GEPA uses execution traces to propose prompt revisions and maintains candidates with complementary strengths. Its mechanism differs from storing a memory for retrieval: experience changes the procedure used on future inputs. Weight adaptation provides another route. SEAL trains a model to generate data and instructions for its own parameter updates; in its single-passage knowledge-incorporation experiment, learned self-edits yield 47.0% accuracy compared with 39.7% for untrained self-generated data on 974 questions from 200 held-out passages. These results establish distinct ways to turn feedback into reusable changes, but their different tasks, resource budgets, and evaluation units do not support a ranking of external state against weight updates. [GEPA, Algorithm 1 and §4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19457v2); [SEAL, Table 2 and Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10943v2).

Other methods change how learning opportunities and updates are produced. R-Zero co-evolves a challenger that generates problems and a solver trained on the resulting curriculum. STOP and Hyperagents make the improvement code itself editable, while Dream-RSI optimizes an exploration controller using recorded discovery histories. These mechanisms raise a stronger question than whether the current agent becomes better at a task: does experience produce a better procedure for improving future agents? A transferred skill, a better discovered solver, and a transferred improvement strategy answer different parts of that question. [R-Zero, §2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.05004v4); [STOP, Algorithm 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02304v3); [Hyperagents, §3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19461v1); [Dream-RSI, §3](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14858v1).

These approaches can also operate within one system. Shopify's August 2026 account of Sidekick describes harness search, repair of unsuccessful trajectories, weight training, and prompt compression in a production pipeline. It provides a concrete example of how experience can move between external procedures and model parameters. However, the article does not provide public ablations or longitudinal quality measurements that isolate the contribution of continual learning. It therefore informs system design more directly than it establishes a causal performance advantage. [Shopify Engineering, “Sidekick's continual learning loop”](https://shopify.engineering/sidekicks-continual-learning-loop).

## What the evaluations establish

The clearest evidence for persistent learning comes from comparisons that retain or remove experience while holding the task stream and backbone fixed. FinEvo-Bench evaluates four scaffolds over streams of 120 financial tasks, using three task-order permutations. Retaining state improves mean rubric scores by 9.33–19.37 points relative to paired resets. Yet its static-skill comparison also shows how much can be achieved without continued updating: for one scaffold, a fixed expert skill scores 86.67, compared with 89.47 for unrestricted evolution and 71.58 for reset state. Together, these results support learning recurring professional procedures while leaving the incremental value of autonomous updating over strong static expertise unresolved. The main effects lack across-run uncertainty intervals, and the tasks share benchmark-defined procedures. [FinEvo-Bench, §§4.1–4.3 and Tables 3 and 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06144v1).

Additional inference is another consequential alternative. Rethinking the Evaluation of Harness Evolution for Agents finds that parallel sampling outperforms the tested harness evolution under a five-rollout budget. In a separate experiment with disjoint search and evaluation tasks, the evolved harness scores 68.3 against 67.7 for the initial harness on 34 held-out tasks. This challenges the interpretation that gains observed during evolution necessarily reflect a reusable improvement in harness design. It does not establish that harness optimization generally fails: rollout counts do not equalize total cost, and a useful harness might recover its development cost over many future tasks. The appropriate comparison therefore depends on the workload over which an update will be reused. [Rethinking the Evaluation of Harness Evolution for Agents, Tables 1–3 and §4.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12227v2).

Persistence also introduces the possibility that later learning will undo earlier gains. AgentStream evaluates six benchmarks with 50 tasks each across three models, five methods, and three order seeds. Its interleaved setting yields a mean improvement of 0.90 percentage points across the tested configurations, alongside both gains and regressions. R-Zero provides a complementary example in weight-based learning: its two-model system's math score falls from 49.12 at step 45 to 46.52 at step 60. These findings concern different protocols and should not be pooled, but both show why the best checkpoint is an incomplete account of a learning system. Neither study isolates a universal cause of deterioration; interference, changing curricula, and unreliable feedback require separate tests. [AgentStream, Tables 2 and 11–13](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00155v1); [R-Zero, Appendix D, Table 6, and Appendix E](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.05004v4).

Evidence for improving the improver is promising but narrower than sustained recursive acceleration. Hyperagents evaluates continued improvement after transferring agents to a new domain, although the reported mathematical-grading endpoint of 0.640 versus 0.610 is not statistically significant across five runs. Dream-RSI reports useful discovery gains against a fixed exploration policy, but its replay guarantee applies to recorded history, and transfer of a discovered solver does not by itself demonstrate general transfer of the controller. These studies make meta-improvement experimentally concrete. Establishing a broadly reusable improvement procedure still requires repeated transfer tests and accounting for the resources used to develop that procedure. [Hyperagents, §5.3 and Figure 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19461v1); [Dream-RSI, §§3–4](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14858v1).

## Implications and open questions

The strongest alternative interpretation is that much of the observed benefit comes from specialization and additional search. The static-skill and sampling comparisons make that explanation credible, while paired persistence effects and learned-update comparisons show why it cannot simply dismiss all gains. The available evidence does not establish how much each explanation accounts for across the field. Our position is consequently conditional: retained learning is useful when its benefit survives the alternatives appropriate to the application, and its value must be assessed over the work that follows the update.

A discriminating experiment would compare a strong static procedure, reset state with additional inference, retained state with a fixed improver, and retained state with an evolving improver. All four would share an initial model, task stream, feedback access, and total resource budget. After development, evaluation would measure performance on new task families and a later time period, alongside earlier-task retention, failures, and search, training, inference, and judging costs. Independent runs and an independently designed evaluator would help distinguish a reusable improvement from selection noise or adaptation to a particular rubric. Superiority of the evolving improver over the fixed improver would strengthen the case for meta-improvement; sustained acceleration would additionally require increasing improvement efficiency over successive cycles. This is a proposed test, not a result of the reviewed work.

The resulting research agenda concerns both the production of useful changes and the conditions under which those changes remain useful. Memory and skill systems must select, retrieve, and retire experience; weight-based systems must incorporate new information while preserving prior capability; meta-improvement systems must demonstrate that their learned strategies travel beyond the histories that selected them. Across these approaches, the unresolved issue is whether accumulated experience produces reliable gains on future work at an acceptable lifetime cost. The literature reviewed through September 16, 2026 supports bounded progress toward that objective, but does not yet establish sustained, domain-general recursive improvement.
