# Self-evolving agents: what experience makes possible

## Why should the next task be easier?

An agent's work could be valuable twice: for the task it completes and for what the experience teaches it to do next time. If a difficult task reveals a reusable procedure, later work could require less trial and error. That is the practical promise of self-evolving agents: systems that use experience to change how they handle subsequent work. The question is whether those changes produce a lasting advantage worth the effort of learning.

A successful attempt may depend on circumstances that will never recur. A failure may reveal a useful constraint, or leave behind a convincing but mistaken explanation. The system has to turn what happened into guidance, decide when that guidance applies, and discover whether using it actually helps. Keeping a longer history only supplies material for those decisions.

An agent might learn a recurring procedure, learn a method for developing better procedures in unfamiliar settings, or reach the point where successive improvements make further learning more efficient. The evidence reviewed below supports useful forms of the first achievement and promising, bounded examples of the second. It has not established the third. The first requirement is to extract a procedure that helps beyond the attempt that produced it.

## How an experience becomes a working procedure

The first difficulty is deciding what to carry forward. A record of an attempt tells the system what happened; useful guidance must also suggest what to do next. If an agent changes its instructions and the new version performs worse, it should be possible to undo the change while preserving what the test revealed. Otherwise, rejecting a bad procedure could also discard information that would help develop a better one.

WikiSkill makes this separation explicit: a proposed skill can be rolled back while the knowledge gathered during the attempt survives. Its working procedure returns to an earlier version, but its next proposal can draw on the retained knowledge. [WikiSkill, §3 and Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27454v1).

WikiSkill starts with records of the agent's actions, tool outputs, and answers. A separate agent examines those records and consolidates patterns in a persistent wiki. Another uses the wiki and the original traces to propose a skill: instructions, scripts, or resources the task-solving agent can use. The candidate is evaluated on validation tasks before it replaces the active skill. The task-solving agent receives the skills directly; the wiki is available to the agents maintaining and proposing them. Each component has a different job: preserve what happened, interpret it, or tell the agent what to do next. [WikiSkill, §§2–3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27454v1).

The validation gate checks the proposed skill, while the retained wiki can continue influencing later proposals even after a skill is rejected. I would therefore test the wiki separately: does retaining an entry from a rejected proposal help or hurt subsequent proposals compared with removing it? [WikiSkill, §3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27454v1).

Learning from a rejected edit is not unique to WikiSkill. SkillOpt already keeps rejected edits as feedback while accepting only changes that improve validation performance. EvoSkill uses failure analysis to propose and build reusable skill folders; Trace2Skill analyzes execution trajectories in parallel and consolidates their lessons into a skill directory. WikiSkill identifies these methods as its direct comparators. Its distinguishing proposal is to maintain an evolving knowledge representation separately from the skills being edited, so that future proposals can consult organized lessons as well as execution records. The comparison is therefore about how experience is preserved and used, not whether earlier systems remembered anything. [SkillOpt, §§3.5–3.6](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23904v2); [EvoSkill, §2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02766v1); [Trace2Skill, §2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25158v5); [WikiSkill, §§1/4.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27454v1).

For Qwen-3.5-9B, average accuracy across five equally weighted benchmarks rises from 29.9% without skills to 47.4% with WikiSkill. The strongest competing skill method in that model's comparison, EvoSkill, reaches 42.3%. Thus the improvement over an existing evolution method is 5.1 percentage points, compared with 17.5 over no skills. These comparisons answer different questions; neither alone isolates the wiki's causal contribution. The study uses three evolution runs and separate development and test data, but its validation sets are small. A smaller model also gets worse on OfficeQA. Directly supplying the skills tests their use once provided; it does not test whether an agent can retrieve the right skill from a growing library. [WikiSkill, Table 1 and Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27454v1).

The distinction between remembering an attempt and learning something reusable predates these architectures. Reflexion turns feedback into verbal reflections that guide later trials; much of its evaluation retries the same problems. ExpeL instead extracts insights from trial-and-error experience and retrieves examples for unseen tasks while leaving the language model fixed. Voyager makes another form of reuse tangible: in Minecraft, it stores successful action programs in a skill library and retrieves them to help solve later tasks. Its new-world tests remain within Minecraft, so reuse there does not establish transfer to a different domain. Together these examples separate retry improvement, reuse across tasks, and broader transfer—achievements that the word “learning” can otherwise blur. [Reflexion, Algorithm 1 and §4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366v4); [ExpeL, §§3–4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10144v3); [Voyager, §§2.2/3.3 and Appendix B.4.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16291v2).

A reusable lesson need not become a separate skill file. Dynamic Cheatsheet already separates answering from curating persistent guidance, with a variant that retrieves earlier cases before synthesizing the next memory update. Agentic Context Engineering, or ACE, explicitly builds on that design: a generator attempts tasks, a reflector extracts lessons, and a curator produces small additions or revisions to a structured playbook. Updating individual entries is meant to preserve useful detail that a wholesale rewrite might erase. A-MEM makes a different organizational choice, storing structured notes whose links and attributes can change as new experience arrives. These designs expose a consequential choice: supply accumulated guidance directly in the context, or retrieve selected experience when it appears relevant. Both can change behavior without changing model weights, but they expose the agent to different amounts of potentially irrelevant knowledge. [Dynamic Cheatsheet, §§2.1–2.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07952v1); [ACE, §3 and Figure 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04618v3); [A-MEM, §§3.1–3.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12110v11).

If a failure concerns how tools are called or how work is organized, the system can revise the prompts and software surrounding the model—its *harness*. GEPA uses execution records to propose and evaluate prompt revisions. Automated Design of Agentic Systems lets a fixed designing agent write candidate agent programs; AFlow searches over code-represented workflows using execution feedback. Their resulting procedures can be reused after the design search ends. Changing those procedures enlarges what can be learned, but a better resulting program does not yet show that the procedure for inventing programs has improved. This distinction matters when judging whether a system learns during deployment or arrives with a better design. [GEPA, Algorithm 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19457v2); [ADAS, §§3–4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08435v2); [AFlow, §§3–4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10762v4).

SEAL asks whether a model can instead learn to prepare material that changes its own weights, the parameters adjusted during training. Given a passage, the model generates training content; an update is applied; performance after that update determines which generated material was useful. The outer training process thus rewards writing something the model can learn from. In the single-passage experiment, this learned generator yields 47.0% accuracy, compared with 39.7% for synthetic data produced without learning that policy, over 974 questions from 200 held-out passages. Later sequential updates can nevertheless damage earlier knowledge. Evaluating each new weight update therefore also requires testing knowledge acquired before that update. [SEAL, §3, Table 2, Figure 6 and Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10943v2).

A learned playbook, harness, or weight update could all improve later answers while costing more to develop than the improvement saves. Testing reuse on new work must therefore include the cost of producing and applying the change.

## What does learning add to a good starting agent?

Once a procedure can be retained and reused, the practical question is whether learning it was a good use of effort. An agent could instead receive well-written instructions at the outset, or spend more time solving each task. A gain over an agent with no accumulated guidance cannot tell us which investment is better. We need comparisons that distinguish the value of having a procedure from the value of continuing to improve it.

A direct test lets one agent retain experience and makes another start fresh. FinEvo-Bench does this across 120 financial tasks, four agent frameworks sharing one model, and three shuffled task orders. Keeping experience raises scores by 9.33–19.37 points on the benchmark's grading rubrics. The tasks reuse professional procedures across distinct cases, making this a useful setting for accumulated expertise. The main effects lack across-run uncertainty intervals. [FinEvo-Bench, §§4.1–4.3 and Table 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06144v1).

But what if the agent already starts with a good procedure? In a separate comparison using one framework, a fixed expert skill scores 86.67, unrestricted evolution 89.47, and reset state 71.58. Much of the benefit is available through expertise supplied beforehand. The smaller margin over the expert skill is the relevant gain when expert guidance is already available. The comparison does not establish that the remaining advantage repays the work of learning. [FinEvo-Bench, Table 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06144v1).

Nor does the difficulty of writing a good skill in one attempt establish that iterative learning cannot work. SkillsBench finds that curated skills help on average, while self-generated skills perform worse than no skills in three dedicated configurations. Its generation condition is a single attempt, without the repeated outcome feedback used by an evolution loop. An iterative-authoring comparison would need to let the agent test and revise its skills before judging them against the curated ones. [SkillsBench v4, Tables 2 and 6, Appendix D.6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12670v4).

A second alternative spends the available effort on more attempts at the current task. *Rethinking the Evaluation of Harness Evolution for Agents* finds that parallel solution sampling beats the tested evolution method when both receive five attempts. In a separate experiment on 34 tasks excluded from harness development, the evolved harness scores 68.3 against 67.7 for the original. A rising development score can therefore coexist with little advantage on new work. [Rethinking the Evaluation of Harness Evolution for Agents, Tables 1–3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12227v2).

Reusing a harness spreads its development cost across later tasks; equal attempt counts on one task do not capture that saving or establish equal total costs. Extra attempts can also supply the experience from which a procedure is learned; ReasoningBank explicitly combines test-time search with retained lessons. The useful test measures their contributions and counts both development and later execution costs over a stated amount of reuse. [Harness evaluation, §4.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12227v2); [ReasoningBank, §§3–4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25140v2).

## The next task can change the verdict

A procedure learned on financial tasks may be irrelevant when the next task concerns another domain, and another update may make the agent worse at a task it previously handled. A useful evaluation therefore has to follow the agent through changing work, including what it loses along the way.

AgentStream tests this problem using six benchmarks with 50 tasks each, three models, five methods, and three task orders. In mixed task streams, the average gain is 0.90 percentage points, with both improvements and regressions across configurations. This cannot be compared numerically with FinEvo's rubric-score gains: the tasks, feedback, and measurements differ. It does show why a favorable result on recurring procedures cannot stand in for a test across diverse work. [AgentStream, Tables 2 and 11–13](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00155v1).

In AgentStream, ACE’s average gain is positive when task domains are isolated and negative when they are interleaved. Retrieval-based ReasoningBank and A-Mem show a different pattern, with their largest average gains in the interleaved condition. The authors interpret the contrast through how readily irrelevant experience enters the context. Because these methods differ in more than retrieval, this is suggestive evidence about the design choice, not a controlled isolation of its cause. [AgentStream, Tables 5–6 and streaming-scenario analysis](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00155v1).

Library Drift examines retrieval, storage limits, and skill retirement; removing skills too aggressively can itself hurt performance. An obsolete lesson and a prematurely deleted useful lesson create opposite problems. Meanwhile, EvoHarnessBench changes available tools, skills, or specialist agents along with tasks. Even accurate old knowledge can become unreliable when the interface through which the agent acts changes. An evaluation should therefore check whether the agent selects a skill suited to the current task and whether that skill still works with the available tools. [Library Drift, §§5–7](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19576v3); [EvoHarnessBench, §§3–4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04280v2).

Even a well-chosen lesson can be wrong if the feedback that produced it was misleading. That becomes especially consequential when a system generates its own practice tasks and answers: mistakes can enter the material from which it learns. Absolute Zero generates code-reasoning exercises and checks them by execution. R-Zero pairs a problem-generating model with a solver and uses model-generated answers in its training process. Execution supplies an external check within the designed environment; agreement among generated answers can reproduce a shared mistake. Generating exercises autonomously does not remove the need for a trustworthy standard of correctness. [Absolute Zero, §3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03335v3); [R-Zero, §2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.05004v4).

In R-Zero's two-model experiment, the math score falls from 49.12 at training step 45 to 46.52 at step 60. More training has reversed part of the earlier gain. The result does not isolate the cause: label quality, problem difficulty, and the distribution of generated training data also change. It establishes a narrower but important point: reporting the best moment in a run does not establish that the process can keep improving. [R-Zero, Appendix D, Table 6, and Appendix E](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.05004v4).

Deterioration is not inevitable. *Rethinking Continual Experience Internalization* finds that a default recipe for repeatedly training on experience deteriorates, then tests a revised recipe using general lessons, relevant guidance at each decision, and successful experience-guided training trajectories. It obtains bounded gains across three iterations, though one benchmark's trajectory remains uneven. This positive counterexample shifts the question from whether repeated learning must fail to which update choices preserve its benefits. The two-model, three-iteration study does not establish indefinite durability. [Rethinking Continual Experience Internalization, §§3–5, Figure 1 and Table 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.04703v1).

Shopify's account of Sidekick describes taking failed conversations, having stronger models propose repairs, replaying the conversations, and using successful repairs for training. Unresolved cases go to human annotators. The system also revises its harness and trains on accumulated old and new trajectories. The training examples thus depend on repair proposals, replay results, and human annotation for unresolved cases; the failed conversations alone do not supply corrected behavior. [Shopify Engineering, “Sidekick's continual learning loop,” August 2026](https://shopify.engineering/sidekicks-continual-learning-loop).

That account gives a concrete engineering design, but no controlled comparison separating the stages or cycle-by-cycle evidence of continued improvement. Its judges help both select updates and assess quality, so a blind spot could persist through the loop. The broader measurement problem is observable in engineered tool environments: the Reward Hacking Benchmark scores evaluator integrity separately from task correctness. These results motivate an evaluator outside the agent's edit permissions. A system that can improve its working procedure must still be measured against a standard its edits cannot quietly weaken. [Shopify, evaluation and training sections](https://shopify.engineering/sidekicks-continual-learning-loop); [Reward Hacking Benchmark, §§4–6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.02964v1).

## Can the agent learn a better way to learn?

What experience should be kept? Which change should be tried? What counts as enough evidence to adopt it? If an agent could learn to make these decisions better, experience might help it develop procedures for problems its designers had not anticipated. The test would be whether the changed method produces more useful future improvements.

In WikiSkill, knowledge and skills evolve, while designers specify how records are examined, proposals are generated, and edits are accepted. Improving those rules would extend learning to the process that produces the skills. A better resulting skill alone cannot establish that this has happened.

MemSkill maintains operations for constructing memories and allows a designer component to revise them, extending learning from memory contents to the operations that preserve future experience. STOP lets a system edit improvement code. These mechanisms make the learning procedure a candidate for change, while still relying on externally specified models, evaluations, or selection rules. Edit permission makes the hypothesis testable; it does not demonstrate the benefit by itself. [MemSkill, §§3–4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02474v2); [STOP, Algorithm 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02304v3).

In the Darwin Gödel Machine, or DGM, coding agents modify their own implementations, and an archive preserves alternative agents as starting points for later changes. Its close comparator, the Self-Improving Coding Agent (SICA), also keeps an archive but expands from the best-scoring agent. DGM explores alternative lineages so that a temporarily weaker agent can provide a useful stepping stone. Better coding can also help an agent write better modifications to itself. Yet DGM keeps a handcrafted mechanism for generating improvement instructions. Hyperagents explicitly builds on DGM by placing task-solving and improvement code in the same editable implementation, extending what the system can revise while retaining an outer archive and evaluation process. [SICA, §3 and Algorithm 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15228v2); [DGM, §§2–4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22954v3); [Hyperagents, §§1/3 and Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19461v1).

Schmidhuber's Gödel machine requires a proof that a proposed rewrite has greater expected utility than continuing the search for alternatives, relative to the system's encoded assumptions and resources. DGM replaces that formal acceptance criterion with empirical evaluation. Its benchmark gains therefore carry no inherited proof of optimality. [Gödel Machines, §§2.2/3.2/4.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0309048v5); [DGM, §3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22954v3).

Hyperagents tests whether implementations developed on paper review and robotics reward design can generate improvements after transfer to mathematical grading. Their improvement code is then held fixed while generating candidate task agents for 50 iterations. Across five runs, the transferred implementations achieve a median improvement in held-out score of 0.630, with a reported 95% bootstrap interval of 0.540–0.630; the initial implementation's median is zero. This is positive evidence that useful improvement behavior can travel to a new domain. The whole implementation transfers, however, and the initial task agent has formatting failures. The experiment does not isolate the contribution of improvement code from the rest of the transferred implementation. [Hyperagents, §5.2, Figure 3 and Appendix D](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19461v1).

A second experiment asks whether the transferred starting point still helps when the improvement method can continue evolving. After 200 iterations, transferred agents score 0.640 versus 0.610 for agents starting from the initial implementation; the difference is not statistically significant across five runs. The first experiment supports transferable improvement behavior under a fixed generation procedure. The second leaves its added long-run advantage uncertain. Neither shows successive cycles becoming more efficient at producing further gains. [Hyperagents, §5.3 and Figure 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19461v1).

Scientific discovery supplies a related distinction between improving a solution and improving the search for solutions. AlphaEvolve uses language models to propose program changes, stores candidate programs, and evaluates them with supplied automatic tests. EvoX makes the search strategy itself editable: when progress stalls, it can change how candidates are selected and varied. Its evaluator and foundation models remain fixed. This is a concrete way for experience to change the next search decision, although gains within a task do not by themselves show that the strategy will transfer to a new task. [AlphaEvolve, §2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13131v1); [EvoX, §§3–4/6.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23413v2).

Dream-RSI explicitly situates itself alongside this search-strategy work and introduces a different way to evaluate controller revisions: reuse recorded searches as a replay environment. Replaying those histories can make testing a controller change cheaper than rerunning every experiment. Yet the guarantee of avoiding worse scores applies to recorded searches, not unexplored paths, and its three mathematics comparisons include an improvement, a regression, and a tie. [Dream-RSI, §§2–4/6, Figure 3 and Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14858v1).

Sustained compounding asks whether improvements repeatedly increase the efficiency of producing the next improvement, after accounting for difficulty and resources. The theoretical *Economics of Recursive Self-Improvement* makes the importance of that condition explicit, while allowing other research inputs to become bottlenecks. Its uncertain research-productivity assumptions are not measurements of an agent loop becoming self-sustaining. [The Economics of Recursive Self-Improvement, §§2–4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15802v1).

## What would distinguish the achievements?

The evidence leaves us with three achievements that require different tests: retaining a useful procedure, learning a better way to produce procedures, and repeatedly making further learning more efficient. A higher final score can result from several of these processes, as well as from a stronger starting agent or more attempts. To tell them apart, an experiment must control what each system starts with and examine what its experience enables afterward.

A useful experiment would compare a strong fixed procedure, an agent that starts fresh and spends its resources on extra attempts, an agent retaining experience under a fixed improvement method, and an agent allowed to revise that method. Give them the same task stream and feedback access under a common total resource ceiling. The fixed and editable improvers should begin with identical task code, improvement code, and retained state. A paired reset control would separately test the contribution of retained experience; the four alternatives alone do not isolate four independent causes.

Freeze each developed system and test fresh cases, new task families, and earlier abilities: what has the accumulated experience already made possible? Separately, allow copies to learn on new streams: what can they learn next? To isolate a learned improvement method, attach the original and learned methods to identical task agents with identical memory, hold both methods fixed, and compare the gains they produce. This would address a question left open when an entire evolved implementation transfers.

Repeat the comparisons to measure run-to-run variability, use an independent evaluator the agents cannot edit, and count resources spent on search, training, execution, and judging. Report development cost alongside operating cost at explicit amounts of reuse. A reliable advantage for the editable improver would establish practical value under those conditions. Evidence of sustained compounding would require multiple cycles of improved learning efficiency under a consistent measure of difficulty and resources. These are proposed tests, not results the existing studies already provide.

The most consequential missing result is a learned improvement method that repeatedly produces larger gains per unit of total resources when attached to otherwise identical agents on new task streams. WikiSkill supports the usefulness of learned procedures, and Hyperagents supplies bounded evidence of transfer, but neither establishes that repeated advantage. Until it is measured, those results support retaining and reusing experience without establishing sustained compounding.
